## Human Desire

- Infants are tied to their parents (primary care takers). Infants often attempt to win their parents love. Parents not always tell infants clearly what they want they confine themselves to telling infants what they do not want, punishing infants after the fact for faux pas. Infants seek to decipher their parents likes, dislikes, and wishes: "What is it they want?" "What do they want from me?"
- In our attempt to decipher their wants, we are confronted with the fact that people do not always mean what they say, want what they say they want, or desire what they demand […] Our parents’ desire becomes the mainspring of our own: we want to know what they want.
- Two same siblings could interpret their parents differently

## Development of Desire

- Infant wants mOther (milk...)
  - If you please mom than you get milk
  - Like sexual pleasure
- Father role cuts the bond between infant and mOther and gives infant another "Other" (Religion, culture, society, sport..."
  - "infant in some examples sees that mOther has sex with father. Infant can't please mOther like father.
- Father makes clear that mOther belongs to him
- At first, the desire is simply for the mother. Then the stylized father (role) tells it that it can’t always have the mother. Instead of a reward signal based on pleasing its mother, it gets a reward signal based on pleasing the abstract concept of The Law (sometimes reified as Dad, God, the moral law, or the set of reasonable people).
- The child constantly desires the phallus ("object a”), the shadowy object of desire that would finally make it whole. What is object a? #Lacan says that “all desire is the desire of the Other”, which is supposed to mean that our desires are:
  - To attain the kinds of things that other relevant people desire
  - to be the kind of thing that other relevant people desire
- It’s impossible to get object a; if you get the thing that you were using as object a before, you won’t enjoy it, and you’ll just come up with a new object a.
- Paternal function: father (or some stand-in) says that your desire for your mother is kind of creepy, and instead of doing that you should follow the rules and be an upstanding member of society instead.
  - Strong paternal function -> good father
  - weaker paternal function -> absent or abusive father

## Core Concepts

| Term              | Explanation |
|-------------------|-------------|
| **Desire**        | Rooted in lack, never fully satisfied. We desire not the object itself but the *object-cause* of desire. |
| **Fantasy**       | The frame we use to relate to *objet petit a* and make our desire livable. |
| **Big Other**     | Symbolic authority (language, norms, law) that structures our world and dictates what is desirable. |
| **Objet petit a** | The unattainable object-cause of desire. Once achieved, a new one emerges. |
| **Small other**   | Our ego's mirror image or rival; shapes self-perception and jealousy. |

## Lacan's Three Orders

| Order         | Description                                         | Example                                  |
|---------------|-----------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **Symbolic**  | The world of language, law, and social roles.       | Calling someone "father", obeying rules. |
| **Imaginary** | The realm of images and ego-formation.              | Identifying with your reflection.        |
| **Real**      | What cannot be symbolized or understood; traumatic. | Sudden loss, pure pain, gaps in meaning. |

## Example Cycle of Desire

- A person wants **status** (influenced by the Big Other).
- He fantasizes that an **expensive car** will fulfill this.
- The car becomes the *objet petit a*.
- Once bought, it no longer satisfies → a new *objet petit a* appears (e.g., a promotion).
- The cycle continues, sustained by **lack** and structured by the **Big Other**.

## Root of lack

The root of lack is the child’s separation from the mother, experienced when entering language and social structure (the symbolic order). Through this, the child loses the sense of original wholeness and can no longer access pure, unmediated satisfaction. This structural loss creates a permanent gap—lack—which fuels desire throughout life.
