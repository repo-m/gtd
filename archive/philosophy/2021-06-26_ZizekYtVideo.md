## 2023-06-04 Summary

1. Zizek argues about a dichotomy of belief and action, suggesting that even when people may not believe in ideologies intellectually, their actions often suggest otherwise. This duality is exemplified in the way societies, such as Yugoslavia, handle ideologies.

   - Conservative leaders never applaud themselves, while Communist leaders applaud themselves because they see themselves as part of the group, serving the ideology.
   - Paradoxes are present even in seemingly monolithic structures like the American army, which bombs lands with bullets containing plant seeds, or in individuals like Gates, a capitalist who is also a human activist.
   - Ideologies are so pervasive that even those who intellectually reject them often act according to their dictates.
   - This concept is illustrated by Bohr's joke about a horseshoe bringing luck whether one believes in it or not.
   - Practices such as the Tibetan monks writing prayers on a wall, thus 'objectively' praying regardless of their thoughts during prayer time, exemplify this separation of belief and action.
   - In Yugoslavia, if someone fully and genuinely believes in the ideology, they are considered dangerous. This highlights the dual role of ideology as both a guide to action and a potential source of threat.
   - **Summary:** Žižek often highlights the concept of "ideological fantasy," suggesting that our actions are driven not just by what we consciously believe, but by subconscious fantasies and desires shaped by ideology. He has frequently referred to Jacques #Lacan 's theories on psychoanalysis to explain this concept.
   - see also [Interplay of Ideology and Subconscious Actions](../projects/HumanScience/2023-06-04_Zizek_InterplayOfIdeologyAndSubconsciousActions.md)

2. In today's society, Zizek sees ideologies being used more to kill hope than to offer it. He also states that the manipulation of truth and data, and the proliferation of alternative facts, are instrumental in shaping and enforcing ideologies.

   - The primary purpose of ideologies today is not to provide hope, perspective, or idealistic images, but to extinguish hope.
   - The assertion that 'nothing would get better' even with organized attempts to change the status quo demonstrates the hope-killing effect of ideologies.
   - The use of 'Opium and People/Populism' as a new form of 'opium for the people' indicates a shift in the social function of religion/ideology.
   - There is a 'death of truth', with data being manipulated to serve ideological purposes. This creates multiple versions of 'truth', undermining the possibility of objective facts.

3. #Zizek emphasizes that certain aspects of life, such as religious faith or love, are not chosen based on intellectual reasoning or argument, but rather on identity and experience. This perspective also extends to his understanding of free choice, which he suggests is more about self-determination than freedom to choose among alternatives.

   - The true religious person does not believe in God because of the arguments but because he is a Christian/Muslim etc. Only a religious person can enumerate why they believe in God.
   - The same applies to love: people don't fall in love because of specific qualities like beautiful eyes. They enumerate these reasons only once they are already in love.
   - Free choice is not about choosing between alternatives (like cakes in a shop) but about choosing who you are - a choice that is not truly free. Falling in love exemplifies this concept: it is sudden and unchosen, and any attempt to 'choose' to fall in love is a deception.







2. **Ideologies today serve more to obscure and distort truth, rather than provide hope or vision.**
   - Žižek is known for his critique of "postmodern" ideologies that claim to be beyond ideology or to have transcended the traditional left-right political spectrum. He argues that such ideologies actually function to maintain the status quo and to obscure the fundamental conflicts and inequalities in society.

3. **Authentic belief and genuine choice are more complex than they appear.**
   - Žižek often explores the notion of "interpassivity," a term coined by Robert Pfaller, which suggests that we can delegate our beliefs or desires to others, or to symbolic objects or rituals, thus complicating our understanding of what it means to truly "believe" or to "choose." This perspective adds a new layer of complexity to our understanding of religious belief and the notion of free choice.
